Some romance novels are set anywhere. The city is a postcard and the love story could happen in Chicago or Paris or Mumbai with nothing changing. Then there are the novels where the setting is load-bearing: where the streets, the families, the food, the politics, the exact particular way two people from this culture navigate desire and expectation, none of it could be moved.

These are the best romantic novels where India is not just a backdrop. It is the point.

---

## [The Zoya Factor](/books/the-zoya-factor) by Anuja Chauhan

<BookCover slug="the-zoya-factor" />

The Indian cricket team. A World Cup. A lucky charm who works in advertising and has no idea what she has walked into. Set during the most watched sporting event on the subcontinent, with a Delhi that feels completely lived in.

**Why the setting matters:** Cricket in India is not a sport, it is a religion. Chauhan uses this to enormous comic and romantic effect. The stakes of the love story are calibrated to the stakes of the tournament, and it works perfectly.

---

## [2 States](/books/2-states) by Chetan Bhagat

<BookCover slug="2-states" />

A Tamil girl from Chennai and a Punjabi boy from Delhi fall in love at IIM Ahmedabad. Getting their families to agree involves navigating every regional, linguistic, and class division that India runs on.

**Why the setting matters:** This novel is a comedy of Indian geography. The Punjabi family and the Tamil family are both drawn with affectionate precision, and the culture clash is the entire plot. Completely untranslatable to any other country.

---

## [Battle for Bittora](/books/battle-for-bittora) by Anuja Chauhan

<BookCover slug="battle-for-bittora" />

An election campaign in a small UP constituency. The heroine is running against her ex. The setting is chaotic, dusty, corrupt, funny, and utterly specific to the Indian electoral experience.

**Why the setting matters:** Indian democracy as a romantic backdrop. The rallies, the caste calculations, the family pressure, the heat. Chauhan makes you love this country even when it is exhausting, which is probably the most Indian feeling there is.

---

## [Those Pricey Thakur Girls](/books/those-pricey-thakur-girls) by Anuja Chauhan

<BookCover slug="those-pricey-thakur-girls" />

A joint family in 1980s Delhi, five daughters, one landline telephone, and the whole city changing around them. Set against Doordarshan, the Emergency&apos;s aftermath, and a Delhi that no longer exists.

**Why the setting matters:** Chauhan is a social historian as much as a romance writer. The period detail here is extraordinary, and the romance means more because it is embedded in a world rendered this precisely.

---

## [The Palace of Illusions](/books/the-palace-of-illusions) by Chitra Banerjee Divakaruni

<BookCover slug="the-palace-of-illusions" />

The Mahabharata retold from Draupadi&apos;s perspective. The loves, the loyalties, the war. Set in a mythological India that feels more real than most realistic fiction.

**Why the setting matters:** This is Indian mythology used as a romantic framework rather than a backdrop. The love story is inseparable from dharma, from duty, from the specific way desire and obligation are understood in this tradition.

---

## [A Suitable Boy](/books/a-suitable-boy) by Vikram Seth

<BookCover slug="a-suitable-boy" />

Post-partition India, four families, the first general election, and Lata Mehra choosing between three very different men. Nearly 1500 pages of a country being born, with a love story at the centre that refuses easy resolution.

**Why the setting matters:** Seth&apos;s India is so dense and particular that the romance could not exist anywhere else. The question of who Lata should marry is also a question about what kind of country India will become.

---

## [The God of Small Things](/books/the-god-of-small-things) by Arundhati Roy

<BookCover slug="the-god-of-small-things" />

Kerala in the late 1960s, the Syrian Christian community, the shadow of the caste system, and a love that crosses a line that cannot be uncrossed. The Booker Prize winner is literary fiction with a devastating romance at its core.

**Why the setting matters:** The Love Laws, as Roy calls them, are specific to this place and time. The tragedy is not generic heartbreak; it is the particular violence of a society enforcing its own rules about who is allowed to love whom.

---

## [The Secret Wish List](/books/the-secret-wish-list) by Preeti Shenoy

<BookCover slug="the-secret-wish-list" />

Diksha is trapped in a conventional South Indian middle-class marriage, comfortable but slowly disappearing. A chance encounter opens something she had closed years ago.

**Why the setting matters:** Shenoy is precise about the specific pressures on women in urban Indian families, the expectations that come with being a good wife, a good daughter-in-law, a good woman. The romance is a form of resistance.

---

## [Almost Single](/books/almost-single) by Advaita Kala

<BookCover slug="almost-single" />

Aisha Bhatia is a 29-year-old hotel manager in Delhi, fielding pressure from every direction to get married, and increasingly unconvinced that she wants what she is supposed to want.

**Why the setting matters:** This is a very Delhi novel. The city&apos;s social geography, its class anxiety, its particular version of modernity crashing into tradition, all of it shapes the love story. Kala writes contemporary urban India with a sharp eye.

---

## [I Too Had a Love Story](/books/i-too-had-a-love-story) by Ravinder Singh

<BookCover slug="i-too-had-a-love-story" />

Ravin meets the woman he is going to marry through a matrimonial website. The love story that builds is warm, specific, and completely unprepared for what comes next.

**Why the setting matters:** Singh captures the particular experience of the Indian arranged marriage process being slowly transformed by online connection. The cultural specificity is not window dressing; it is what makes the story hit.
